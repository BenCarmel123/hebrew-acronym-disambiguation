\section{Introduction}
Hebrew acronyms are common in news, parliamentary and everyday text. Usually written with a quotation mark before the last letter, as in \heb{ח״כ} (\heb{חבר כנסת}, ``member of parliament''), they often have several meanings. For example, \heb{מט״ח} can mean \heb{מטבע חוץ} (foreign currency) or \heb{המרכז לטכנולוגיה חינוכית} (the educational technology center). Context must distinguish these meanings. Hebrew morphology and orthography add difficulty: prefixes such as ``in'', ``to'' and ``the'' attach directly to words, while acronym dictionaries must accommodate evolving usage \citep{jacobs2020acronyms}.

A language model can resolve an acronym by writing its expansion freely or by selecting from supplied candidates. Generation permits answers outside a dictionary; selection constrains the answer space and depends on the available entries. We ask how these formats compare on the same Hebrew occurrences, and how a small task-trained Hebrew encoder compares with prompted language models at selection.

Our contribution is an assembled data and evaluation pipeline linking candidate inventories, contextual examples, saved model responses and transparent scoring. We compare three open LLMs, including the Hebrew-specialised DictaLM, four proprietary LLMs and two uses of DictaBERT: pretrained similarity and task-trained candidate ranking. The test set combines natural parliamentary and Wikipedia text with authored examples that broaden sense coverage (Section~\ref{sec:data}). The analysis notebook reproduces the saved-result comparisons without new model calls.

The trained encoder is competitive with the tested local Qwen configurations, while API models score higher. For all six main LLMs, selection exceeds generation under the automatic metrics. These findings concern the tested configurations and answer formats: reference wording, unequal scoring rules and imperfect labels limit stronger conclusions. DictaLM is reported separately because of a saved input-template limitation.
