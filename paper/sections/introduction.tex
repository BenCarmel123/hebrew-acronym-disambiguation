\section{Introduction}
Hebrew acronyms are common in news, parliamentary and everyday text. Usually written with a quotation mark before the last letter, as in \heb{ח״כ} (\heb{חבר כנסת}, ``member of parliament''), they often have several meanings. For example, \heb{מט״ח} can mean \heb{מטבע חוץ} (foreign currency) or \heb{המרכז לטכנולוגיה חינוכית} (the educational technology center). Context must distinguish these meanings. Hebrew morphology and orthography add difficulty: prefixes such as ``in'', ``to'' and ``the'' attach directly to words, while acronym dictionaries must accommodate evolving usage \citep{jacobs2020acronyms}.

A language model can resolve an acronym by writing its expansion freely or by selecting from supplied candidates. Generation permits answers outside a dictionary; selection constrains the answer space and depends on the available entries. We ask how these formats compare on the same Hebrew occurrences, and how a small task-trained Hebrew encoder compares with prompted language models at selection.

We build a data and evaluation pipeline that links a candidate inventory of 2,702 expansions for 638 acronym types, contextual sentences, saved model responses and transparent scoring. The test set has 381 items, mostly natural parliamentary and Wikipedia sentences, with authored examples that broaden sense coverage (Section~\ref{sec:data}). We compare seven LLMs, three local (two Qwen models and the Hebrew-specialised DictaLM 2.0) and four API models, with two uses of the Hebrew encoder DictaBERT: pretrained similarity and task-trained candidate ranking. An analysis notebook reproduces every comparison from the saved responses.

We find that:
\begin{itemize}
\item a 184M-parameter encoder trained on fewer than 3,000 examples reaches 71.7\% in selection, above Qwen 7B and DictaLM 2.0 and close to Qwen 14B, while the API models score higher;
\item selection beats generation for every LLM, by 31 to 58 points under the automatic metrics, although automatic matching underrates generation;
\item the Hebrew-specialised DictaLM 2.0 stands out in generation: it generates the reference four times as often as Qwen 7B of similar size, but gains only 5.3 points in selection.
\end{itemize}
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses what these results do not show.
