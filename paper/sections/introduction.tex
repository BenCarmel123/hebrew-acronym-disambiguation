\section{Introduction}
A Hebrew acronym is a short written form that can represent more than one expression. The task in this study is to supply the expansion of a particular occurrence in a sentence. Recognizing the letters is insufficient when several expansions are possible; the system must relate the marked occurrence to the surrounding text. Orthographic variation and attached prefixes also matter because the target seen in a sentence need not be identical to a dictionary entry.

There are two practical ways to ask a language model for an expansion. In \emph{free generation}, the model receives the sentence and target and writes an answer. In \emph{candidate selection}, it also receives a list of expansions and returns the identifier of one entry. Generation can express an answer missing from the list, but it can also produce an unsupported expansion or identify a referent without expanding the acronym. Selection restricts the response space and makes unfamiliar candidates available. It therefore depends on the quality and coverage of the inventory, as well as the model's ability to choose appropriately.

Our research question is: \emph{How does performance on the same Hebrew acronym occurrences differ between free expansion and selection from an inventory?} We compare the two formulations within each of two language models, Qwen and Gemini. A Hebrew encoder, DictaBERT, provides a complementary supervised selection system. It scores sentence--candidate pairs after task-specific training. The available train and dev files separate exact stored acronym forms, but not all morphological families. All five arms receive the sentence and the same identified target occurrence. There is no condition without the sentence.

This design addresses a choice of task formulation, not a causal explanation of model behavior. The LLM comparison changes the information supplied and the required response, from a phrase to a letter. The encoder comparison additionally changes pretraining, architecture, supervision and decoding. A performance difference cannot be attributed solely to model size, candidate information or use of context. Nor does separation of stored acronym forms establish separation of their morphological families or absence from pretraining.
