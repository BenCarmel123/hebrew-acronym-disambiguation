\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}
\paragraph{Hebrew acronyms.}
HAADS disambiguates abbreviations in Hebrew and Aramaic Jewish Law documents \citep{hacohenkerner2010haads}. \citet{jacobs2020acronyms} build an acronym dictionary for Modern Hebrew from unannotated text and use context to choose among its entries. These works separate two problems that our study also separates: finding the possible expansions of an acronym, and choosing the one a sentence intends. Both treat the second problem as choosing from a dictionary and predate current LLMs. We take the dictionary step as given, building our inventory from Wikipedia, Wiktionary, gematria readings and review-time additions, and study the choosing step with current models. We also ask what happens when a model must write the expansion without any list.

\paragraph{Acronym disambiguation in English.}
SciAD formulates acronym disambiguation as choosing a long form from a dictionary-derived candidate set given the sentence and acronym \citep{veyseh2020acronym}, the same formulation as our selection format. \citet{chen2023gladis} introduce GLADIS, an English benchmark with acronym-disjoint splits, so that test acronyms are unseen in training. Our split by acronym type follows the same principle. Their model, AcroBERT, reads the context and one candidate together and learns to tell matching pairs from non-matching ones. Our encoder follows this pair-scoring idea but uses binary cross-entropy, whereas AcroBERT uses a triplet ranking objective. GLADIS builds many evaluation sentences by replacing full expressions with acronyms, so the acronym was not written by the original author. We use such substituted sentences only for training and test mostly on naturally occurring text. Both works evaluate only selection, so they cannot show how selection compares with free generation.

\paragraph{Hebrew language models and LLM ambiguity.}
DictaBERT is a BERT-style encoder pretrained on Hebrew text \citep{shmidman2023dictabert}, and DictaLM 2.0 is a Hebrew-adapted 7B LLM \citep{shmidman2024dictalm}. We use the first as our encoder and the second as a Hebrew-specialised LLM. The closest LLM study for Hebrew is SemCog Bench \citep{liang2026cognates}, 1,858 Arabic--Hebrew word pairs labeled as true cognates, false friends or loanwords. It tests 16 LLMs, including Qwen2.5-7B and DictaLM 2.0 Instruct, on two three-way multiple-choice tasks: identifying the type of a word pair, and judging whether the Arabic sentence, the Hebrew sentence or both use the word correctly. Models lean on surface-form similarity and often mistake false friends for true cognates. DictaLM 2.0 Instruct scores near chance on both tasks (31.6\% and 32.7\%). Context and model scale help only some models. For example, cognate identification improves for Qwen2.5 up to 14B parameters. Their contextual sentences were generated by an LLM and reviewed by a native speaker, as our authored sentences were, and a second annotator checked 10\% subsets of their word pairs, which our single-reviewer test labels lack. All of their tasks are multiple choice, and they note that this may not reflect open-ended generation. Our generation format addresses exactly this setting.

\paragraph{Answer format and scoring.}
Multiple-choice evaluation is convenient but sensitive to option order and identifiers \citep{zheng2024selection}, so we fix one shuffled order per item and report it as a limitation. Open answers avoid this problem but are hard to score automatically. \citet{agrawal2022large} ask GPT-3 to expand clinical abbreviations without choices, then map generated text to candidates by character overlap, turning generation back into selection for scoring. \citet{meconi2025large} compare word-sense selection with generated definitions and explanations rated by an expert. Strong free-form performance under that protocol shows why string agreement and semantic evaluation must be distinguished. We keep the two formats separate, score generation by reference-string agreement, and measure how far hand judgment departs from it.

\paragraph{The gap.}
To our knowledge, no prior work compares free generation with candidate selection for Hebrew acronyms on the same occurrences, or compares a fine-tuned Hebrew encoder with current LLMs, including a Hebrew-specialised one, on this task. We do both, on a test set that is mostly natural text with acronym forms unseen in training, and measure how much the scoring rule changes generation results.
