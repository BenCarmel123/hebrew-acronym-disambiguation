\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}
\paragraph{Hebrew acronyms.}
HAADS disambiguates abbreviations in Hebrew and Aramaic Jewish Law documents \citep{hacohenkerner2010haads}. \citet{jacobs2020acronyms} build an acronym dictionary for Modern Hebrew from unannotated text and use context to choose among its entries. These are the closest works to ours, and they show that finding expansions and choosing between them are separate problems. They provide a dictionary-based context for our comparison of a task-trained encoder with prompted LLMs. We study expansion in context, with a candidate list supplied only in the selection format.

\paragraph{Selecting from candidates.}
\citet{chen2023gladis} introduce GLADIS, an English benchmark with acronym-disjoint splits. Their model, AcroBERT, reads the context and one candidate together and learns to tell matching pairs from non-matching ones. Our encoder follows this pair-scoring idea but uses binary cross-entropy, whereas AcroBERT uses a triplet ranking objective. GLADIS covers English candidate selection and builds its examples by replacing full expressions with acronyms. It therefore does not answer our generation--selection question for Hebrew naturally abbreviated text. We use mostly naturally occurring test sentences and keep substituted sentences for training.

\paragraph{Judging generated answers.}
A generated answer can be right without matching the reference string, so the way answers are judged changes the score. \citet{agrawal2022large} ask GPT-3 to expand clinical abbreviations without choices, then map each answer to the candidate with the highest contiguous character overlap. This ties the score to the candidate list even though the model generates text. We instead preserve a reference-string inclusion score and report its limits. \citet{meconi2025large} test LLMs on word senses in two ways: selecting among supplied definitions, and generating definitions or explanations that an expert annotator rates by hand. They find that the best LLMs match supervised systems at selection; candidate shuffling lowers average performance, with the largest drop for the smallest model. They also report strong free-form explanations under expert evaluation. Generation is evaluated on a sampled subset with a different scoring procedure, and the task is word senses, not acronyms. We score generated answers automatically against the reference and check a selected subset by hand. We also shuffle the candidate order.

\paragraph{The gap.}
These studies motivate comparing candidate-based resolution with unconstrained answers while distinguishing their evaluation rules. We examine both formats for LLMs on the same Hebrew acronym occurrences, and compare selection with a Hebrew encoder ranker. Our results address this setting rather than a ranking against scores from different languages, corpora or evaluation protocols.
