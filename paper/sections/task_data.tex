\section{Task and Data}
\label{sec:data}
\subsection{The unit of prediction}
Each item contains a sentence $x$, an acronym $a$, a candidate inventory $C$ and a reference answer $y\in C$. For the encoder, a character span $s$ identifies the target text $x[s]$, marked with \texttt{[ACR]} and \texttt{[/ACR]}. In saved test inference this span is derived automatically; LLM prompts name the acronym alongside the original sentence (Section~\ref{sec:systems}).

Generation predicts an answer from sentence context and target information, without candidates. Selection picks one member of $C$ for the same item. We use \emph{expansion} as shorthand for the stored inventory answer: besides ordinary spelled-out expressions, it includes numerical interpretations in gematria (the use of Hebrew letters as numbers) and conventional or named uses that are not literal expansions. For example, the reference for \heb{ח״י} in one test sentence is \heb{שמונה עשרה בגימטריה} (eighteen in gematria; Table~\ref{tab:res-examples}). Evaluation therefore measures agreement with this inventory and its reference labels, not only recovery of the words that formed an acronym. We retain all such items and labels.

The output spaces are:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\hat{y}^{\mathrm{gen}}&=f(x,t)\in\Sigma^{*},\\
\hat{y}^{\mathrm{sel}}&=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{c\in C}\mathrm{score}(x,t,c),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $t$ denotes the target information and $\Sigma^{*}$ is the set of text strings, so generation may return any string, while selection returns one candidate in $C$.

\subsection{Sources and construction}
Candidate expansions come from Hebrew Wikipedia abbreviation and disambiguation pages and from Wiktionary. We merged the two sources and reviewed near-duplicate expansions by hand, which left 2,702 candidate rows for 638 acronym types. Sentences come from Hebrew Wikipedia article text and from the Knesset plenary corpus (HaifaCLGroup/KnessetCorpus). We kept only sentences of 40 to 200 characters with a clear acronym boundary, allowing attached Hebrew prefixes, and we removed sentences with glosses, explicit definitions or other acronyms. A dictionary entry does not show that a sense occurs in context, so candidate sources and sentence labels are kept separate.

Sentences are of four kinds. \emph{Natural} sentences contain the acronym as written. \emph{Substituted} sentences come from Wikipedia: a spelled-out expansion was replaced with the acronym, and the removed expression is a weak label. \emph{Deglossed} sentences are natural sentences with an adjacent explanation removed. \emph{Claude-authored} sentences were written by an LLM to add senses to types that had few. Substituted and authored sentences give labels cheaply, but they need not read like naturally abbreviated text.

\paragraph{Train.} The current encoder training file contains 2,829 items, which give 12,684 sentence--candidate pairs. Its exact identity at historical training time is not recovered. Most items are substituted, so most labels are weak. Stored labels were reused without comprehensive reannotation.

\paragraph{Development.} The research development set holds 62 natural Knesset items for 11 types and supports encoder checkpoint selection and development comparisons. It is distinct from both the execution pilot and test cohort. Its labels combine reused human reviews and AI-assisted author decisions, rather than independent annotation.

\paragraph{Test.} The test set has 381 items for 60 types. Human-review evidence is documented for all 253 Knesset items; the 87 Claude-authored items carry a human-review provenance marker. The 41 Wikipedia items have a stored verified status but no recorded review verdict, so the available records do not establish human review of every test label. The Knesset review compared labels with the candidate list: 221 test labels were confirmed and 32 corrected. The 87 Claude-authored items add three sentences each to 29 types whose reviewed Knesset examples all shared one sense. We removed 14 Knesset items from four protocols that also supply training items, leaving no shared recorded document key between test and the documented train/dev files. No inter-annotator agreement estimate is available. This label provenance is distinct from the review of model responses in Section~\ref{sec:eval}.

\subsection{Separation of the sets}
\label{sec:separation}
Preparation checked item identity, target boundaries and candidate structure. These checks show structural validity, not that a label is correct. In the documented train, development and test files, exact acronym strings and sentence strings are disjoint across sets. This is a property of those files, not a reconstruction of the historical training inputs or a claim about pretraining exposure. Recorded document keys (source and title) are disjoint between test and train/dev, but train and dev share three keys (three train and five dev items). Prefixed variants of one acronym can also fall in different sets: train has \heb{מד״ר} and dev has \heb{בד״ר}. The split therefore does not show generalization to wholly unseen acronym families.
