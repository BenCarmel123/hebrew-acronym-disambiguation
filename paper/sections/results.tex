\subsection{The task-trained encoder}
\label{sec:res-encoder}
The encoder is competitive with the tested local Qwen configurations and close to Qwen 14B, while the four API systems score higher. Its recorded selection count is 273/381 versus Qwen 14B's 266/381. A separate post-hoc sensitivity check substitutes contextual references for three known errors (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}): strict selection then gives 274 versus 269. The primary table retains the historical labels. Neither this small gap nor the single-run design establishes stable superiority.

The pretrained similarity baseline scores 245/381 (64.3\%), versus 273/381 for the ranker, a difference of 28 items (7.3 points). This is not an ablation of training: separate context/candidate vectors and cosine similarity are replaced by joint encoding and a learned scoring head. The results do not establish that fine-tuning matters more than size or that Hebrew pretraining caused an advantage. Likewise, the Qwen 7B and 14B runs compare two model configurations, not a controlled intervention on size.

Like AcroBERT \citep{chen2023gladis}, the ranker scores context--candidate pairs, permitting candidates for forms absent from task training. It evaluates each candidate separately; latency and memory were not measured.

\subsection{Generation versus selection}
\label{sec:res-formats}
Selection scores exceed generation scores for each of the six LLMs under the task-specific rules (Figure~\ref{fig:generate-vs-select}b).

The inventory contains every stored test reference (100\% oracle coverage), partly through review-time additions that included test-related review. Generation receives no candidates and is checked by string inclusion; LLM selection receives candidates and must return a valid label. The gap therefore does not isolate the gain from candidates or semantic correctness under a common metric.

A post-hoc diagnostic finds parentheses in 67/381 references. Across six LLMs these 67 shared items yield 1/402 automatic generation matches versus 339/402 selection matches: 402 response occurrences, not independent items. References include meta-descriptions and numerical glosses. For \heb{י״ח} in \texttt{kn4-0499}, Grok generated \heb{שמונה עשר (18)} (eighteen), while the reference is \heb{שמונה עשרה (גימטריה)} (eighteen, gematria). The string rule rejects it; review marks it \texttt{fits}. Parentheses do not make matching impossible or define semantic invalidity, but the observed mismatch illustrates sensitivity to reference wording. On the remaining 314 items the automatic direction persists for all six systems (Gemini: 207 generation versus 294 selection matches). This post-hoc subset does not replace the full-cohort comparison.

The selective review records 338 automatically rejected generation responses labeled \texttt{fits}. These are diagnostic response occurrences judged against displayed references, not global semantic accuracy or a common human ranking. The established selection--generation ordering is limited to automatic metrics.

Selection returns an explicit inventory entry, while generation can express valid answers outside the list or in a different form. This practical distinction does not establish semantic superiority. In word-sense disambiguation, \citet{meconi2025large} also find strong free-form explanations under expert evaluation. In clinical abbreviation expansion, \citet{agrawal2022large} map generated text back to candidates before scoring. Their scores are not directly comparable to ours; comparing answer correctness requires a common semantic evaluation.

\subsection{Results by kind of sentence}
\label{sec:res-source}

The overall difference between similarity and the trained encoder is concentrated in Knesset items: 146 to 177 correct out of 253. It is 31 to 30 out of 41 for Wikipedia and 68 to 66 out of 87 for authored sentences. This limits generalization of the aggregate improvement. The groups also differ in acronym and sentence composition, so the differences do not identify an effect of source.

\subsection{DictaLM and measurement}
\label{sec:res-measurement}
The separate DictaLM results show why the scoring distinction matters (Table~\ref{tab:dictalm-supplement}). Automatic generation accepts 125 responses and author annotation (B.C.) accepts 193. They agree on 121 accepted responses; 72 are accepted only by the author, four only automatically and 184 by neither. Thus the annotation identifies disagreement with the string rule in both directions, without establishing a uniform semantic metric for the other models.

All 381 selection responses fail strict label-only decoding. Reading a leading label yields 243 reference matches, but this is a diagnostic of saved responses, not a replacement result. The template mismatch described in Section~\ref{sec:eval} further limits comparison; we cannot attribute the behavior to that mismatch or to Hebrew specialization.

\subsection{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
Our framework compares ways of answering the same contextual acronym question, rather than treating model choice alone as the experimental variable. A task-trained Hebrew encoder is competitive with the tested local Qwen configurations when candidates are available; API models score higher under the recorded rules. This supports considering candidate ranking when an inventory is available, but provides no measured efficiency advantage or causal attribution to training, size or Hebrew specialization.

Selection exceeds generation under the automatic metrics, yet candidate access and reference design shape what counts as success. An inventory can expose a conventional interpretation whose wording a free answer would not reproduce. The selective reference-relative review cannot supply a common semantic evaluation, and known reference errors make close rankings fragile. A next evaluation should therefore validate references independently and judge generated and selected answers under the same semantic criterion.

Two focused future tests could clarify these issues. Newly coined acronym--meaning mappings in informative contexts could probe contextual interpretation versus familiar associations; coinage alone cannot prove absence from pretraining, and arbitrary mappings require contextual evidence. A separate test could vary candidate-set size, plausible hard distractors and option order while retaining the correct answer and a common semantic score. Our lists already include wrong candidates and a random reference. Since chance is $1/k$ for an item with $k$ candidates, changing $k$ changes both chance and difficulty; adding distractors alone would not isolate a statistical effect.
